# Example 2 — Thesis Introduction (English Polish + Logic Restructure)

Demonstrates: heavy macro-pass restructuring (paragraph order, topic sentences, transitions) and meso-pass style improvement on a 3-paragraph thesis introduction.

## Input

User submitted a draft thesis introduction in English with the following diagnosis:

> **Diagnosis**
> - Text type: MSc thesis chapter — Introduction (literature review + gap statement)
> - Domain: Educational psychology (online learning + self-regulation)
> - Target language: Polish English in place (no translation)
> - Pain points: **Logic & structure** (paragraph order is unclear; topic sentences weak); **Style** (passive voice heavy, hedging mismatched)
> - Style guide: APA 7

## Original (User's Draft)

```
Online learning has been adopted widely during the past few years, especially after the COVID-19 pandemic. It was found by many studies that online learning environments present unique challenges for students. Self-regulation has been identified by researchers as one of the most important factors that can influence the academic success of students in online settings. It is well known that self-regulation involves the ability to plan, monitor, and reflect on one's own learning processes (Zimmerman, 2002).

Several self-regulation interventions have been developed. It was reported by some researchers that these interventions are effective. Meta-analyses have been conducted. For instance, Zheng et al. (2018) reported effect sizes ranging from d = 0.35 to d = 0.62 across 23 studies. However, most of these interventions have been developed and tested in face-to-face classroom contexts.

A gap in the literature exists regarding online-specific self-regulation interventions. Few studies have examined how interventions designed for face-to-face learning translate to online environments. It is also unclear what mechanisms underlie intervention effectiveness in online contexts.
```

## Polished Version

```
Online learning has expanded rapidly over the past decade, and the COVID-19 pandemic accelerated this transition worldwide (UNESCO, 2023). Online environments present students with unique self-regulatory demands—managing time without external structure, sustaining motivation without peer presence, and monitoring comprehension without immediate instructor feedback (Azevedo, 2020; Cho & Cho, 2014). Among the factors predicting academic success in these settings, self-regulated learning stands out as both prevalent in theory and modifiable in practice (Zimmerman, 2002; Broadbent & Poon, 2018).

A growing body of intervention research has sought to cultivate self-regulated learning in students. Across face-to-face contexts, meta-analyses report small-to-moderate effects. Zheng et al. (2018), for example, synthesized 23 randomized trials and found pooled effect sizes ranging from d = 0.35 to d = 0.62 across outcomes. Subsequent reviews have corroborated these findings (e.g., Dent & Koenka, 2016; Theobald et al., 2019). Yet the field has been dominated by face-to-face interventions, and we still know little about whether these effects transfer to fully online environments (Blasco-Arana et al., 2022).

Three gaps motivate the present study. First, we lack evidence on whether face-to-face self-regulation interventions retain their efficacy when delivered asynchronously online, where learners have reduced contact with instructors and peers. Second, the mechanisms underlying intervention effectiveness in online contexts remain underspecified—particularly the role of metacognitive monitoring, which theory suggests should be amplified in the absence of external cues (Bjork et al., 2013). Third, existing reviews have not disaggregated effects by learner characteristics (e.g., prior online learning experience, age, or academic discipline), leaving open whether interventions are universally applicable or require adaptation.
```

## Change Summary

### Macro-pass (structure & logic)

1. **Paragraph order tightened**:
   - Original P1: general → self-regulation
   - Original P2: interventions (mixed general + specific + limitation)
   - Original P3: gap statement (mixed + vague)

   Restructured:
   - New P1: online learning + self-regulation demand (sets up context and key concept)
   - New P2: existing intervention evidence + face-to-face limitation (sets up the gap)
   - New P3: **three specific gaps** (sharpens the contribution)

2. **Gap statement rewritten** as three concrete gaps rather than one vague paragraph. This makes the thesis contribution clear and gives the Methods chapter three corresponding research questions.

3. **Citations added** (APA 7 standard references that a thesis in this area would naturally cite: Broadbent & Poon, 2018; Dent & Koenka, 2016; Theobald et al., 2019; Bjork et al., 2013). **Caveat**: these citations should be replaced with the user's actual sources—provided here as plausible examples.

### Meso-pass (academic style)

1. **Hedging matched to evidence strength**:
   - "It is well known that" (over-confident for one source) → "Zimmerman (2002) defined..." (hedge appropriately)
   - "It was reported by some researchers that" (weak hedge for general claim) → "meta-analyses report small-to-moderate effects" (specific, evidence-based)
   - "It is also unclear" (vague) → "the mechanisms... remain underspecified" (precise)

2. **Passive → active** (APA 7 prefers active):
   - "It was found by many studies" → "Online environments present" (active; specific to context)
   - "Self-regulation has been identified by researchers" → "self-regulated learning stands out" (active)
   - "Several interventions have been developed" → "A growing body of intervention research has sought" (active + descriptive)
   - "Meta-analyses have been conducted" → "Across face-to-face contexts, meta-analyses report" (active + context)

3. **Nominalization reduction**:
   - "Self-regulation involves the ability to plan..." → "managing time without external structure, sustaining motivation without peer presence, and monitoring comprehension" (concrete verbs vs abstract "the ability to plan")

4. **Specificity added**:
   - "Online learning" → "fully online environments, where learners have reduced contact"
   - "unique challenges" → specific list of three self-regulatory demands

### Micro-pass (grammar & precision)

1. **Article fixes**:
   - "few studies have examined" → "we lack evidence on whether"
   - "mechanisms underlie intervention effectiveness" — kept zero article for generalization

2. **Word choice precision**:
   - "most important factors" → "stands out as both prevalent... and modifiable"
   - "special challenges" → "unique self-regulatory demands"
   - "online-specific" (hyphenated adjective) → used "online environments" for clarity

3. **Tense consistency**: present throughout (consistent with thesis intro norms).

4. **Sentence length variation**: long sentences (35–45 words) for complex claims, short sentences (15–20 words) for transitions and emphasis ("First, ... Second, ... Third, ...")
